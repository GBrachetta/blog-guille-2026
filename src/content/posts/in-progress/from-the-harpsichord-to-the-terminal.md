Whenever people find out that I am both a harpsichordist (and conductor) and a web developer, there is always a brief pause while they try to make the two things fit in the same sentence.

On the surface, it does look a bit schizophrenic. In one half of my life, I am surrounded by wood, quills, 18th-century treatises, and arguments about how fast a French overture should go in 1720. In the other half, I am looking at TypeScript errors, React components, Django migrations, and database queries.

People usually assume that music is the emotional, romantic side and programming is the cold, logical side. In my experience, it is almost the exact opposite.

## The score as a codebase

A Baroque score is one of the most structured, logical systems you will ever meet.

When you are preparing a Bach fugue at the keyboard, or standing in front of an ensemble rehearsing a Handel concerto grosso, you are essentially reading a complex architecture with multiple voices running in parallel. Every line has its own job, its own timing, and its own dependencies on the other lines.

Conducting a rehearsal is surprisingly close to debugging a distributed system (except the servers have feelings, hold conservatory degrees, and occasionally get offended if you tell them they are twenty milliseconds late). You listen to the whole texture, isolate which layer is causing the friction, change one small thing in the articulation or balance, and run the loop again to see if the phrase finally locks into place.

## Code is more creative than it looks

Conversely, writing software is much less mechanical than people outside tech think it is.

Yes, the syntax is strict and the compiler has zero sense of humour. (Neither did J.S. Bach, for that matter.) But once you get past the basic mechanics, building something for the web has the same satisfaction as shaping a piece of music: taking something complicated under the hood and making it feel effortless to the person on the other side.

Whether it is putting together a full-stack application in Next.js or Django, or making a page respond cleanly without unnecessary clutter, you are constantly making choices about rhythm, structure, and proportion.

## The best of both centuries

What I enjoy most is that each world gives me a break from the worst habits of the other.

After a day of chasing a bug caused by a library that updated itself overnight, sitting down at an acoustic instrument whose technology hasn't needed a firmware update since 1750 is a tremendous relief. You press a key, a plectrum plucks a string, and no cloud server is involved.

And after hours of practicing a difficult passage that still refuses to cooperate because my fourth finger has its own opinions, there is something deeply comforting about writing a function, running it, and watching it do the exact same thing every single time.

Most days the two worlds stay in their own rooms. Every now and then (like when I built [Musical Dice](https://gbrachetta.github.io/Musical-Dice/)), they meet in the middle. Either way, living with one foot in the 18th century and the other in the terminal keeps things interesting.
