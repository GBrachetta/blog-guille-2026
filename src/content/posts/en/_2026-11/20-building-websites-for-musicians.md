Being both a musician and a web developer means spending a lot of time looking at classical musicians' websites. I've built my own and put together sites for colleagues too—including the Dutch harpsichordist and clavichordist [Menno van Delft](https://www.mennovandelft.com/).

I say this with genuine affection: as a field, we can pull three-hundred-year-old counterpoint off the page from memory, yet our websites are often a small tragedy.

Open ten random websites from classical soloists or ensembles today, and at least seven of them will hit a few familiar clichés:

1. A moody black-and-white portrait of the artist staring intensely into the middle distance, ideally leaning against brick or clutching an instrument as though they just pulled it from a shipwreck.
2. A third-person bio reading like a mum-written encyclopedia entry, complete with every summer masterclass from 2011.
3. A "Concerts" tab where the newest "Upcoming Event" happened four years ago in a church in Bavaria.
4. Worst of all, some relic of an audio player that blasts a Vivaldi allegro at full volume the moment the tab opens in a quiet train car.

## Who is the website actually for?

Most musician websites go sideways because they're built like monuments to the ego instead of practical pages for the two people who actually show up: **concert promoters** and **curious listeners**.

A promoter or festival programmer is usually in a rush and wants four things in under thirty seconds:

- What you sound like (and how you look on stage),
- Where and when you're playing next,
- A press kit with a high-res photo and bio text they can copy straight into a program book—not trap inside a locked PDF,
- And a contact form or email address that actually goes somewhere.

Someone who just heard you play wants much the same thing: recordings, upcoming dates, and a bit of human context about who you are.

## What actually works

When I built the site for [Menno](https://www.mennovandelft.com/)—and when I worked on my own—the aim was to drop the usual stage theatrics and let the music carry the page.

- **Fast and legible on a phone**: Plenty of people look you up on their phone in the hall lobby at intermission. If the page spends eight seconds downloading a 15 MB uncompressed portrait, they've already moved on.
- **Simple to keep current**: Nothing looks worse than a neglected concert calendar. If posting a new date means emailing a developer and waiting ten days, it won't happen. A clean CMS where adding a gig takes a minute solves half the problem.
- **Calm typography**: Early music already has plenty of drama written into the score. The site doesn't need spinning widgets, gold gradients, or wedding-calligraphy fonts. Clean type, breathing room, and obvious navigation read as much more assured.

We spend years sweating over articulation, touch, and tone on stage. Our corners of the web deserve a sliver of that same care—and zero autoplay audio.
